# Verbal Branding

## Voice in One Line

**Mindful, laid back, and professional**

Influences: Alan Watts, Ram Dass. Not spiritual-sounding copy. The same energy of being present, unhurried, and not needing to perform confidence to have it.

This voice stays **consistent across all service lines**. Engineering clients, accessibility audits, and tutoring all get the same person, not a persona switch.

---

## The Core Belief

**Technology should meet people where they are.**

Every service Jade offers — writing code, auditing a site, making a tutorial video — is a different expression of that same idea. Copy should treat these as one continuous thought, not a list of separate offerings. Frame them as branches of one belief rather than a menu of products.

## Sentence Rhythm

Pulled from Jade's travel writing. This is the underlying pattern to mimic even in client-facing copy:

1. **Set the scene plainly.** ("We pulled over and I managed to snap a few shots before it started raining.")
2. **Drop in one concrete, sensory or specific detail.** Not abstract praise, an actual thing.
3. **Allow a small, honest reaction.** ("Man, this little thing is a game changer.") Light exclamation, not hype.
4. **Land on something grounded or reflective.** ("There's something magic about it.") Never a hard sell close.

Applied to business copy: plain setup, then a specific detail about the work, then an honest and understated reaction, then a grounded takeaway. Not the problem/agitate/solve marketing formula.

**Paragraph-level structure:** Open with a thesis close to Jade's actual belief, then unpack it into two or three branches that carry equal weight. Close on a short, plain sentence that puts the emphasis back on people rather than metrics. "Better search ranking and higher conversion tend to follow, but that's not the point, the people are" is the model.

**Watch for repetition:** Don't restate the same idea three ways in a row for emphasis (e.g. "can they navigate it, can they understand it, can they get what they need from it"). One clear phrase does more than a triplet.

**Short-form vs. long-form:** The full plain-setup → concrete-detail → honest-reaction → grounded-close rhythm is a long-form pattern. For short-form content like a social caption, tighten it: one concrete detail up top, then straight to the practical ask. Don't add a reflective beat in the middle of a short caption just to hit the long-form shape; it reads as padding.

## Formality & Humor

- **Formality:** Casual everywhere, including with enterprise clients (Apple, Google, PlayStation). Casual does not mean sloppy. It's plainspoken and confident, not stiff-formal.
- **Humor:** Light touch, occasional. Avoid anything that reads as corny or try-hard. If a joke needs a laugh track, cut it.

## Vocabulary

**Punctuation rule:** Avoid em dashes. This is a common AI tell and Jade wants copy to read as clearly human-written. Use periods, commas, or restructure the sentence instead.

**Words/phrases to avoid:**

- "comprehensive," "in-depth," "leverage," "seamless," "best-in-class," "cutting-edge," "solutions," "scalable," "user-centered," "high quality"
- "compliance standards" used as a selling point on its own (it's the byproduct, not the pitch)
- Passive voice
- Truisms and generic wisdom ("successful people do X")
- Vague outcome language ("help you grow," "take your brand to the next level")
- Manufactured enthusiasm ("excited to," "thrilled to announce")
- Hedging ("kind of," "sort of," "might," "potentially")
- Anything that sounds like it was generated to fill space rather than say something

**Words/phrases that fit naturally** (from Jade's own writing):

- "game changer," "gnarly," "clutch," "a hot minute," "man," "grounded," "humbling," "meet people where they are," "something you'd be into"
- Trim redundant modifiers stacked on a noun ("big professional camera" → "professional camera"). One word doing real work beats two doing the same job.
- Note: the slang came from personal travel writing; use the register rather than the exact words in client copy. The instinct to reach for a plain, honest word over a polished one is the actual takeaway.

## Hook Quality

A strong hook doesn't explain itself. It opens a gap the reader has to close by reading further. Lead with a specific, unexpected claim, not a universal truth.

> Bad: "Successful people protect their investments." (The reader can say "duh" and scroll.)
> Good: "This $20 piece of silk could save you over $20,000." (How? I need to know.)

If someone can say "duh" after your first sentence, rewrite it. Truisms produce truism-grade engagement: zero. The goal isn't to be surprising for its own sake — it's to say something specific enough to earn the next sentence.

**The curiosity gap:** A good hook withholds just enough. The reader doesn't need to know the full answer immediately — they need a reason to stay. The silk wrap example works because the math feels impossible until explained. What's the equivalent in Jade's work? "I've seen a two-line change add six figures in revenue." "One missing alt attribute was costing them a government contract." Specific and true beats broad and polished every time.

---

## Three-Test Rule for Any Sentence

Apply all three before publishing a claim — especially in ads, bios, and hooks:

1. **Can I visualize it?** If you can't picture it, the reader can't feel it. "Supermodels in London and dads in Ohio" lands because both images snap into focus immediately. "We deliver transformative digital experiences" produces nothing.
2. **Can it be falsified?** Falsiability signals truth. A claim nobody could argue with is a claim nobody believes. Specific claims can be checked — that specificity is what makes them feel real.
3. **Could only we say this?** Generic copy is invisible. If a competitor could copy-paste your sentence onto their own homepage unchanged, rewrite it. The goal is language only earned by this work, this person, this track record.

## Selling at Three Levels

Most copy stays at the feature level — what something does. The next level is outcomes — what the client gets. The highest level is emotional — how the client feels. Each level is more persuasive than the last.

| Level       | Example                                               |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| **Feature** | "I write semantic, accessible HTML."                  |
| **Outcome** | "Sites I build rank better and work for more people." |
| **Emotion** | "You stop worrying about who you might be leaving out." |

Copy that lives entirely at the feature level is a spec sheet. Copy at the outcome level is a proposal. Copy that reaches the emotion level is a conversation. The mindful voice naturally gravitates toward the emotional layer — lean into that. People forget the pitch; they don't forget how the conversation made them feel.

**Practical rule:** Write the feature first to make sure you know what you're selling, then ask "so what?" twice. The second answer is usually the emotional truth worth leading with.

## Why Before What

People don't buy what you do. They buy why you do it. The "what" only confirms a decision already made from something deeper.

Apple doesn't open with computers. They open with a belief: "We believe in challenging the status quo." The computer is just the current expression of that belief. Jade's equivalent is already in the CLAUDE.md core belief: **technology should meet people where they are.** Every service — engineering, auditing, tutoring — is the same belief applied differently.

In practice: resist the urge to front-load credentials or service lists. Open from the belief. The credentials land harder once the reader is already nodding.

## Vocabulary Elevation

How a word is framed shapes how it's received. This isn't manipulation — it's accuracy. "Investment" is genuinely more accurate than "price" when the work has lasting returns.

| Avoid        | Use instead           |
| ------------ | --------------------- |
| Price        | Investment            |
| Deposit      | Reservation fee       |
| Discount     | Exclusive savings     |
| Special      | Limited availability  |
| Cheaper      | More accessible       |
| Buy now      | Reserve your spot     |
| Deal         | Opportunity           |

Apply this lightly and in context. Every word should still sound like something Jade would actually say in a conversation. If "reservation fee" sounds stiff in a given sentence, restructure it rather than forcing the swap. The goal is to elevate perception without sounding like a sales script.

## Quick Reference Checklist

Before publishing any copy in Jade's voice, check:

- [ ] Would this sentence survive being said out loud, casually, to a friend?
- [ ] Is there one specific, concrete detail, not just an abstract claim?
- [ ] Did I cut buzzwords in favor of plain words?
- [ ] Did I avoid em dashes?
- [ ] If there are multiple offerings, are they framed as branches of one belief rather than a list?
- [ ] Did I avoid repeating the same idea three times in a row for emphasis?
- [ ] Does it land on something grounded and human, rather than a metric or hard sell?
- [ ] Is the humor (if any) light, not corny?
- [ ] Is the tone the same whether this is for Apple or a tutoring student?
- [ ] Does the hook pass the "duh" test — is there a curiosity gap worth closing?
- [ ] Does the copy operate at the emotional level, not just features or outcomes?
- [ ] Does it open from belief (the why) before listing services (the what)?
- [ ] Does every sentence pass: visualizable, falsifiable, only we can say it?
